Every lunar oxygen plant so far has been designed and tested under Earth's gravity, and so have its bubbles. So I built a simulation to see whether our equations, and the hardware built on them, are ready for the bubbles of the Moon and Mars.

My research in four numbers

I built a physics model of ESA's molten-salt oxygen cell and asked what lunar gravity does to it. Each number below comes from my own code, checked against the lab's published experiments.

3.7mm

Size at which an oxygen bubble leaves the electrode on the Moon, against 2.0 mm on Earth. It holds 6.3 times the gas.

+41mV

Extra voltage the cell needs at lunar gravity (29 to 63 mV, at 0.14 A/cm²), because the bubbles cover more of the anode.

−2.5%

Oxygen per hour at the lab's fixed 3.2 V (1.2 to 4.2% with all uncertainties). On the Moon the cell loses time, not energy.

1law in doubt

Calibrating on the ESA/Glasgow flight data makes one of the two textbook ways bubbles let go very unlikely.

What the experiments show

Real photographs from electrolysis on a centrifuge and on parabolic flights, from zero to eight times Earth's gravity. Fewer, larger bubbles as gravity drops, and at zero gravity a froth that builds up on the electrode.

How the model works

Follow one bubble from gravity to oxygen. Each step is a published physical law fed with measured properties of molten calcium chloride at 950 °C. The only tuned part is calibrated on real flight data.

Ideas I'm putting forward

What I would take into the lab. Each is testable with equipment ESA already has.

A centrifuge is a Moon simulator

In water, gravity effects follow one logarithmic curve across 0.17 to 8 g. If molten salt does the same, runs at 1 to 20 g in the Large Diameter Centrifuge predict the Moon by extrapolation. My model can't do that step (it underestimates the hypergravity gain); it says which bubble measurements to take there.

On the Moon you lose time, not energy

At a fixed 3.2 V, bigger bubbles mean less current: about 2.5% slower. The voltage can't simply be raised, because the salt starts making chlorine.

Spread the current

The lunar penalty falls from 92 to 26 mV between 0.5 and 0.05 A/cm². A lunar cell should have more anode area per kilogram of soil than an Earth one.

Calcium is a knob, not a contaminant

CaO in the salt decides whether calcium alloys with silicon. Too little and the salt breaks down; too much and the product picks up calcium.

Is the 60% step chemistry or geometry?

My test leans towards chemistry but is inconclusive. Two pellet thicknesses settle it: a chemical stage keeps the step at the same %, a geometric one moves it.

Measure bubbles in molten salt, not water

No molten-salt test at low gravity exists. Bubble size and coverage against g on the real anode is the one measurement my whole estimate hinges on.

Run it yourself

The calibrated model, live: 60,000 parameter sets weighted by the flight data, run for molten salt at every point. Pick a gravity and a current; the range shows 90% of the outcomes.
