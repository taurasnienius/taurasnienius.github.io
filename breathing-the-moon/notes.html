Working model · molten salt electrolysis of lunar regolith

How much Moon do you need to breathe for a year?

I wanted to understand the numbers behind getting oxygen and metal out of lunar soil, so I built this model of the molten salt (FFC) process that ESA has been developing with Metalysis and the University of Glasgow. Every input you can move is either a published value or an assumption I've labelled as one.

Tauras Nienius · Aerospace engineering, ELISA Aerospace (ISAE Group) · November 2026
01

Where the oxygen actually is

Regolith is around 45% oxygen by mass, but almost none of it is in an easy place. It's locked in silicates, mostly in silica. That one fact decides which extraction process is worth building.

Oxide compositions are the XRF values from Exolith's December 2023 spec sheets4, normalised to 100% without loss on ignition.

Share of total oxygen, by oxide

Why this rules out the "easy" route at the south pole. Hydrogen reduction only frees the oxygen bound to iron, mainly in ilmenite. On a highland simulant, temperature-programmed reduction measured about 0.02 wt% oxygen released, against 1.1 wt% for pure ilmenite5. Highland soil has almost no iron. To get at the ~58% of the oxygen sitting in silica, you have to reduce silica, which is what molten salt electrolysis does.

02

The charge is fixed. Everything else is a choice.

Every oxygen ion pulled out of an oxide carries two electrons, whatever metal it was attached to. So the charge per kilogram of O₂ is set by physics: 1000 / 16.00 × 2 × 96 485 C ≈ 12.1 MC, or about 3 350 Ah. The energy depends on two numbers that are engineering choices, not constants: the cell voltage and how much of the current actually removes oxygen.

The voltage has to stay below the point where the CaCl₂ salt itself starts to decompose. Current efficiency drops when calcium dissolves in the melt and carries current without removing oxygen6. Schild et al. measured higher current efficiency on anorthosite than on the full highland simulant2, but I haven't found a number I can quote, so it stays a slider.

03

Why the 25 g prototype is the wrong size to judge energy by

The prototype at ESA's ESTEC processes about 25 g of simulant in 24 hours3. At that size, almost all the energy goes into keeping a small, hot reactor hot. Heat leaks through the surface, but oxygen comes from the volume, so the energy cost per kilogram falls steeply as the batch grows. The chart sweeps batch size with your other settings fixed.

Operating temperature 950 °C, as in Lomax et al.1 The reactor is a cylinder as tall as it is wide, sized to hold the salt and regolith. Heat capacities, the salt's latent heat and densities are approximate textbook values, and the heat-loss coefficient lumps all the insulation into one number. I don't know what a flight reactor would use for either the salt ratio or the insulation, which is exactly why they're sliders.

04

When to stop a batch

In the 2020 run, Lomax et al. had extracted about 76% of the oxygen after 16 hours and 96% after about 50 hours1 (ESA's press release rounds the first point to 75% at 15 h, which is what I fitted). The last fifth of the oxygen takes more than twice as long as the first three quarters, while the reactor keeps losing heat the whole time. So there should be a best time to stop a batch and start the next one. I fitted a simple saturating curve, X(t) = Xmax(1 − e−kt), through those two points. It gives k ≈ 0.100 per hour and Xmax ≈ 96.7%. That's a curve fit to two points, not a kinetic model, and that run was at constant current on loose powder, so its time curve mostly reflects current efficiency. The newer constant-voltage pellet data are analysed in the methods notes.

Uses the batch size, salt ratio, insulation and salt setting from section 03, and the voltage and efficiency from section 02. If the salt stays molten, the reactor keeps losing heat during the turnaround too, which pushes the best stopping time later.

What surprised me: with a small, leaky reactor the energy optimum stops well before the published 96%. Energy isn't the only cost though. Stopping early means digging and handling more regolith per kilogram of oxygen and leaving a half-reduced product that is harder to use.

05

What still has to come from Earth

A lunar oxygen plant only makes sense if it saves more mass than it consumes. Two consumables are easy to forget: salt that leaves with the product each batch, and, if the anode is carbon, the carbon itself, which leaves as CO₂ or CO. Burning carbon to CO₂ uses 12 / 32 = 0.375 kg of carbon per kilogram of O₂ removed. Producing CO uses twice that.

Losses come from salt trapped in the reduced product and from evaporation. The anode setting is the one in section 02.

06

A plant for a real crew

NASA's ECLSS technical brief uses 1.083 kg of oxygen per person per day as an example, including two hours of exercise7. A habitat would also recycle some of its oxygen, so the share the plant has to supply is a slider.

07

What's left behind: more metal than oxygen

Run the process to completion and the residue is every metal that was bound to that oxygen. By mass, that's more than the oxygen itself. If every oxide were reduced, highland simulant would leave about 1.2 kg of metal per kilogram of O₂, mostly silicon, aluminium and calcium.

That residue isn't a clean metal. Schild et al. found a heterogeneous mix of intermetallics, including Al₂Si₂Ca, AlSiCa and Al–Si–Fe compounds, often several phases inside a single particle. They concluded that sieving and elutriation can't isolate specific alloys2. They also found Cr, Mn, Ni and Mo picked up from the reactor itself.

Bars assume every oxide is reduced in the same proportion, which is a simplification. Calcium oxide in particular partly dissolves into the CaCl₂ salt.

08

On an asteroid, the question changes

I'm interested in this process because of asteroids, not only the Moon. Asteroid feedstock is a different problem, because a lot of the iron is already metal and some bodies hold water. The cheapest path to oxygen depends on the body's class.

Ordinary chondrites · H, L, LL

Separate the metal before electrolysis

Total iron is about 28, 22 and 19 wt%, and free metal about 8.4, 4.1 and 2 vol% for H, L and LL8. Metal grains are magnetic, so a magnetic separation step could pull them out first, leaving a cleaner silicate feed and a separate Fe–Ni product.

Carbonaceous · CI

Bake out the water first

Up to 20% water held in clay minerals, plus up to 11% magnetite and up to 6% organic matter9. Heating to drive off water and splitting it is likely a far cheaper first source of oxygen than electrolysing silicates. Electrolysis would come second, on the dry residue.

Enstatite chondrites

Iron that's already reduced

Iron sits only in metal and troilite (FeS), with no iron oxides, and the metal can carry up to 1% silicon9. Oxygen here is all in silicates, so the electrolysis case looks like highland regolith but with a metal stream to handle.

Metal-rich, M-type

The by-product becomes the product

For a body like Psyche, the target is the metal itself. Oxygen would be a by-product of processing whatever silicate is mixed in, which is the reverse of the lunar case above.

10

Questions I'd bring to the lab

  • Is highland regolith actually the easier feedstock? It has almost no iron, but Schild et al. measured higher current efficiency on anorthosite. If that holds, the south pole may be a better place to run this than the mare.
  • Could reduction be staged instead of sieved? Oxides reduce in a rough order of stability: iron first, then silicon, then titanium and aluminium, with magnesium and calcium hardest. If a run were stopped part-way, would the early product be a cleaner Fe–Si alloy? That would move separation from after the process into the process.
  • What sets the batch time on the Moon? On Earth it's the reduction kinetics. At the south pole it might be the power available through periods without sunlight, which would push towards keeping the salt molten and accepting longer batches.
  • How much salt leaves per batch, and can it come back? Section 05 shows this decides whether the plant pays for itself. A washing step that doesn't rely on water shipped from Earth seems like an open problem.
11

What this model leaves out

  • The O₂ handling chain after the anode: drying, compression and storage.
  • Real kinetics. The extraction curve is a two-point fit and doesn't depend on particle size or temperature.
  • Any coupling between current efficiency and how far the batch has gone. In reality, efficiency probably falls as the easy oxygen runs out.
  • Lunar ambient temperature, radiative losses in vacuum, and reactor mass. All heating starts from 20 °C, as in a lab.
  • Sodium and potassium oxides, which may behave differently in the melt.

Corrections are welcome. That's the reason for building it.
