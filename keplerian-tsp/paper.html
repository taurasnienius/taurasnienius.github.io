Deferred-timing search for the Keplerian travelling salesperson problem

Tauras Nienius · Aerospace engineering, ELISA Aerospace (ISAE Group) · November 2026 · technical paper · code

Abstract

The Keplerian travelling salesperson problem (KTSP) asks for the cheapest closed tour, in total Δv, that rendezvous with every body of a set moving on Keplerian orbits, with departure and arrival dates on a time grid [1]. I rebuild the cost model of the benchmark published by ESA's Advanced Concepts Team and verify it by reproducing all 22 published optima for instances with up to 10 bodies (to the printed cent). I then describe a search in which the timing of a partial tour is never fixed: every partial tour carries its whole cost-versus-time curve, which is updated exactly when a leg is added. This gives an exact schedule for any fixed visiting order, a beam search that does not degrade on fine grids, and a local search that always compares optimally timed tours. On the 13 instances with 20 or more bodies the method finds 4 tours cheaper than the best published ones (up to 3.5 % on 80 asteroids and 161 time points) and equals the published value on the other 9. A genetic-programming search over the beam's ranking rule rediscovers the hand-designed rule but does not beat it on held-out instances. The technique of propagating time-dependent cost functions is known from time-dependent routing [7]; the contribution here is its application to this benchmark, the verified results and the negative result on rule discovery.

1Problem

An instance gives $n$ bodies (body 0 is the depot), their Cartesian states at an initial epoch, a gravitational parameter $\mu$ and a time window $[t_0, t_f]$. Time is restricted to a uniform grid of $K$ points $\tau_0=t_0,\dots,\tau_{K-1}=t_f$. A tour is a permutation $\pi$ of the bodies $1..n-1$ together with departure and arrival indices $(d_p, a_p)$ for each of the $n$ legs, with

$$ d_p < a_p, \qquad a_p \le d_{p+1}, \qquad 0 \le d_0, \quad a_{n-1} \le K-1 . \tag{1}$$

Waiting at a body between an arrival and the next departure is free. The cost of the tour is the sum of the leg costs,

$$ J(\pi, d, a) = \sum_{p=0}^{n-1} c\big(\pi_p, d_p, \pi_{p+1}, a_p\big), \qquad \pi_0=\pi_n=0 . \tag{2}$$

I consider tours that visit every body exactly once. The formulation of [1] works on a time-expanded network and also admits repeated visits; this restriction is discussed in Section 10.

2Cost model and its verification

The leg cost is the two-impulse rendezvous cost of a Lambert arc. With $\mathbf r_i(\tau), \mathbf v_i(\tau)$ the two-body state of body $i$ at time $\tau$,

$$ c(i,k,j,l) = \min_{s} \Big( \lVert \mathbf v^{\rm dep}_s - \mathbf v_i(\tau_k) \rVert + \lVert \mathbf v^{\rm arr}_s - \mathbf v_j(\tau_l) \rVert \Big), \tag{3}$$

where $s$ runs over all solutions of Lambert's problem from $\mathbf r_i(\tau_k)$ to $\mathbf r_j(\tau_l)$ in time $\tau_l-\tau_k$: every admissible number of revolutions, both branches, and both transfer directions. Bodies are propagated with pykep.propagate_lagrangian and arcs solved with pykep.lambert_problem. All $c(i,k,j,l)$ are precomputed into a tensor $C$ of size $n \times K \times n \times K$ (about 1.3 GB for $n=80$, $K=161$; 7 minutes on two cores).

The paper [1] does not need to spell out these choices because it distributes the time-expanded networks. I did not use those files; I identified the model by brute force on the smallest instances. Multi-revolution arcs are required: on Jovian moons 4/11 the optimum is 33 268.31 m/s with zero-revolution arcs only and 20 096.59 m/s with all revolutions, the published value. Allowing the retrograde direction changed nothing on the instances I tested and is kept for completeness.

Exact solver. For small $n$ a dynamic programme over (visited set $S$, current body $j$, time index $t$) is exact:

$$ F(S \cup \{m\}, m, l) = \min_{j \in S} \; \min_{k \lt l} \Big[ \min_{k' \le k} F(S, j, k') + c(j,k,m,l) \Big], \tag{4}$$

with $F(\{0\},0,\cdot)=0$ and the closing leg back to body 0. It runs in $O(2^n n^2 K^2)$; 10 bodies and 161 time points take 1.6 s. Table 1 compares it with every optimum published in [1] for $n \le 10$.

3Exact timing of a fixed order

Fix the order $\pi$. Let $A_p(t)$ be the cheapest cost of the first $p$ legs such that the spacecraft is at body $\pi_p$ and free to leave at grid index $t$. Because waiting is free, $A_p$ is non-increasing in $t$. Then $A_0 \equiv 0$ and

$$ A_p(t) = \min_{l \le t} \; \min_{k < l} \Big[ A_{p-1}(k) + c(\pi_{p-1}, k, \pi_p, l) \Big]. \tag{5}$$

This is a min-plus product of the vector $A_{p-1}$ with the $K \times K$ slice of $C$ for that pair of bodies, followed by a running minimum. The optimal cost of the order is $A_n(K-1)$ and the dates follow by backtracking. One leg costs $O(K^2)$, a whole tour $O(nK^2)$: about 20 µs per leg for $K=161$ in compiled code.

The same recursion run backwards gives $B_p(t)$, the cheapest cost of legs $p..n-1$ when the spacecraft is at $\pi_p$ and free to leave at $t$. For any position $p$,

$$ J^*(\pi) = \min_t \big[ A_p(t) + B_p(t) \big]. \tag{6}$$

Equation (6) makes local search cheap: if a move changes the order only between positions $lo$ and $hi$, the prefix curve $A_{lo-1}$ and the suffix curve $B_{hi+1}$ are still valid, so the new tour is evaluated exactly with $hi-lo+2$ applications of (5) instead of $n$.

4Beam search with deferred timing

A beam search over (order prefix, arrival index) pairs has to commit to dates as it goes. In [1] the beam search of width 10 000 ends between 19 % and 1,205 % above the best known value on the 13 larger instances, and gets worse as $K$ grows. Here a beam state is a prefix together with its whole curve $A_p(\cdot)$. Expanding a state by body $b$ applies (5) once. Two states with the same visited set and the same last body are duplicates for ranking purposes and only the better ranked is kept. After $n-1$ expansions each surviving state is closed with the leg back to the depot.

What remains is how to rank states, since a curve is not a number. Each child is summarised by eight features (cost features are divided by an instance scale, the median over body pairs of the cheapest leg departing at $t_0$):

FeatureMeaning
a_sched$A_p(t^*)$ at the proportional date $t^* = (p/n)(K-1)$: the cost if the tour stays on schedule
a_min$\min_t A_p(t)$, the cheapest way to have visited the prefix at all
a_late, a_early$A_p$ at $t^* \pm 0.1(K-1)$
legincrease of $\min_t A$ caused by the last leg
depth$p/n$
tmin$\arg\min_t A_p(t) / (K-1)$, the share of the window already used at the cheapest point
nearmean over unvisited bodies of the cheapest leg from the last body departing at or after $t^*$
Table 2. Features available to a ranking rule.

The hand-designed rule is simply a_sched. Ranking by a_min, the obvious choice, favours prefixes that are cheap because they have used most of the window, and leaves no time for the rest of the tour.

5Local search and iterated local search

Neighbourhood: relocation of a segment of 1 to 3 consecutive bodies, swap of two bodies, and reversal of a stretch, all restricted to positions at most $W$ apart ($W$ = 12 to 15). Every candidate is evaluated exactly with (5) and (6). Two descent variants are used: best improvement (for the comparisons of Section 7) and first improvement with a random scan order (for long runs, several times cheaper per improvement on 80 bodies).

Iterated local search perturbs the current tour with 4 random segment relocations inside the same window, descends, accepts the result if it is better (or with probability 0.02 otherwise), and returns to the best tour found with probability 0.01 per iteration. Runs start from the better of two beam-search tours (width 200 or 300) after one descent.

6Baselines

Two of the methods named in ACT's internship topic were implemented on the same evaluator, so that the comparison isolates the search and not the cost model. P-ACO (a plain population-based ant colony optimisation of my own, simpler than the Beam P-ACO hybrid of [3], which I did not reimplement): 20 ants per iteration, population of the 5 best tours, pheromone on body-to-body arcs, $\alpha=1$, $\beta=2$, heuristic desirability from the on-schedule cost of the candidate leg; ants also carry the curve $A$. MCTS: UCT ($c=0.7$) over the next two bodies from the committed prefix, rollouts that complete the order by sampling from a softmin of the cheapest-leg table, each rollout scored exactly with (5), one body committed per step. Both get the wall-clock time of the beam search plus 20 s.

8Automatic discovery of the ranking rule

The ranking rule is the one hand-designed component of the beam search, which makes it a small test case for automated heuristic design [5, 6]. Rules are expression trees over the features of Table 2 and constants, with operators $+,-,\times,\div,\min,\max$, negation, $\sqrt{|\cdot|}$, $\log(1+|\cdot|)$ and square. Genetic programming, in the spirit of symbolic search over expressions [4]: population 200, 40 generations, tournament selection of size 4, 60 % crossover, 30 % mutation, 10 % fresh trees, 5 % elitism, trees of at most 25 nodes, parsimony pressure $2\times10^{-4}$ per node. Fitness is the mean ratio to the best known value of a width-20 beam search on six training instances (10 and 20 bodies). Three independent runs start from random trees; a fourth starts from the hand rule with width 50 on five instances.

All three unseeded runs converge on rules dominated by a_sched, with a penalty on tmin or a product with a neighbouring sample of the curve. On the training set they improve on the hand rule by about 3 % (fitness 1.087 down to between 1.051 and 1.058). On held-out instances the differences are within about 1 % in both directions, and the seeded run, which was allowed to specialise at width 50 on fewer instances, is 3 to 6 % worse. With eight held-out instances a 1 % difference is not significant. I read this as: the feature set already contains the right quantity, a formula search finds it, and there is little left to gain by recombining these features. The improvement that mattered, replacing a date by a curve in the state, is a change of representation that lies outside a search over formulas.

9Verification

  • The cost model is tied to [1] by Table 1.
  • The scheduler (5) was checked against brute-force enumeration of all orders with the exact solver (4) on four instances, and the identity (6) holds to $10^{-6}$ at every position.
  • All compiled routines were run once with array-bounds checking enabled.
  • Every tour in Table 4 was re-evaluated by a separate script that shares no code with the solver: it reads the instance file, propagates the bodies, solves each Lambert arc again, propagates the spacecraft along the arc and measures the distance to the target at arrival ("worst arc miss"), checks constraints (1) and the visit-once rule, and sums the impulses. Totals agree with the solver to $10^{-6}$ m/s. The checker uses the same Lambert library (pykep), so an error inside that library would not be caught.

10Limits

  • Upper bounds only. No optimality proof; the lower bounds are those of [1].
  • Single visits. The formulation of [1] allows a body to be visited more than once. All its published optima for $n \le 10$ are reproduced by single-visit tours, but for larger instances a cheaper tour with revisits could exist.
  • Compute is not comparable. The values of [1] come from a mixed-integer solver with a 4-hour limit on a 2 × 64-core server and include bounds; mine from heuristics on two cores, minutes to 75 minutes, without bounds.
  • One run per instance. The long runs were not repeated with different seeds, so no variance is reported.
  • Benchmark model. Two-body dynamics, impulsive legs, grid dates. Nothing here addresses low thrust.
  • Rule discovery. Genetic programming over fixed features, not a language model; small training and test sets.
  • Memory. The dense tensor $C$ grows as $n^2K^2$; beyond a few hundred bodies it would have to be computed lazily or sparsified.

11Relation to earlier work

Carrying time-dependent cost information along a partial route is a known idea in time-dependent routing; dynamic programming with labels for the time-dependent TSP with time windows [7] is one example, and I do not claim the idea as new. TSP formulations of multi-target space missions go back to at least [2], and the hybrid of beam search and population-based ant colony optimisation for multi-rendezvous trajectories is from [3]. The KTSP benchmark, its exact methods and the B-Swan heuristic are from [1]. What this work adds is the combination applied to that benchmark, a verified independent implementation of its cost model, improved upper bounds on four open instances, and a first, negative, data point on automatic discovery of a search component for it.
